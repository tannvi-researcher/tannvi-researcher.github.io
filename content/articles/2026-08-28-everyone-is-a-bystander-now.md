In October 2024, I saw a [YouTube video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8DEvsaVJwpM) by a bunch of these [undergraduate kids from Harvard](https://idtechwire.com/harvard-students-connect-meta-ray-bans-to-pimeyes-face-search-provoking-privacy-concerns/), who were using Ray-Ban Meta glasses attached to PimEyes. And they go around wearing those glasses, recording people's conversations, and the people they're conversing with don't even have any idea that they've been recorded. And within seconds, I swear, seconds, you can pull out their name, their gender, their background, where they belong, what's their address, what course they have enrolled into, etc.

Now, I did some digging, and it's supposed to be like a project which the kids have done, and it was published by a few of them. But nothing really came out as much to me as this particular case, because it made me realise that it's not about recording. Because people do that all the time on roads, making Instagram videos and YouTube videos and becoming influencers and taking interviews on streets. But with a mobile phone, with a device which is so obviously recording, or at least you have a hunch it's recording you. And on the contrary, people suddenly have a change of behaviour. They start setting their faces in a certain way, speaking in a certain way, get a little cautious the moment they see a device around them, because they realise that it might be recording them, either visually or audio.

Which stood out to me really much, because very recently, on July 20th, [New York courtrooms have banned smart glasses](https://www.engadget.com/2211151/new-york-first-us-state-ban-smart-glasses-all-courthouses/). For the longest time, they had already banned recording inside the courtroom, which is something which almost every court, in their bylaws and guidelines, does. For example, the Delhi High Court has [something very similar in their guidelines](https://www.livelaw.in/news-updates/delhi-high-court-rules-live-streaming-219758) as well. But they don't name smart glasses, unlike the New York court, which has named smart glasses to be particularly banned, because of the non-obviousness and non-deliberateness of them as compared to other recording devices. And the reason they've done that is because, across 1,240 state, county, city, town and village courts, this applies to everyone who's coming there wearing them. All you have to do is surrender them to the court officer. And if your prescription glasses are also your smart glasses, you are just supposed to wear a simple dummy prescription glass.

The simple logic is that the surreptitiousness of recording a proceeding is illegal, and the fact that it's not obvious to somebody who's being recorded stood out for the court. And this objection sounds very clever to me. The fact that why would you want to particularly ban something like a smart glass? Because the non-obviousness, the invisibility of recording, is interesting.

Now, with this background in mind, I started digging a little more, to figure out what's worse. A hidden camera is worse than a visible one? Is that what we're trying to say? Yes. Because at least with a visible camera, though your privacy is being infringed, and of course being recorded is no fun without consent, at least in that sense you are able to pull yourself out and say that I don't want to be recorded, or I will change my behaviour, or I will not say something controversial. Not being aware that you're being recorded creates a different situation in itself.

Now, what stood out for me particularly in all of this is the small LED which is attached to the Meta Ray-Ban glasses. Which stood out as a response from the counter side, the companies who are behind the creation of this, suggesting that the small LED light will switch on when it's capturing. And then I ran a quick search around the internet as to how this was being dealt with. And thanks to Reddit and Quora, I found a bunch of ways to cover that using black stickers. And that made me realise that if you are being creepy, there are ways around that. A good move like this, which enables the signs of recording, can be so easily tampered with. And this is not exactly something which a manufacturer should take pride in.

Now, apart from just the capability of the devices being different from phones, and the obviousness and the observability, what also stood out to me was that this is so funny. Because in February this year, [Zuckerberg and a bunch of his team members walked inside a trial over social media addiction](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/meta-trial-mark-zuckerberg-ai-glasses/), a separate case in itself, with these glasses. And the judge warned them against recording, and was reportedly worried that jurors being captured and identified is dangerous for their own privacy and for their own security. And that is indicative of the audacity of the manufacturers themselves, to undertake such an action inside the court.

Then I did a little bit more digging and found out that it's not just New York which has banned smart glasses, but similar kinds of bans already exist in [Wisconsin](https://www.wiwd.uscourts.gov/news/2025-02) and [Pennsylvania](https://www.aol.com/news/philadelphia-courts-ban-smart-meta-020946371.html) and in [Hawaii](https://www.glassesradar.app/guide/where-are-smart-glasses-banned) courts. So it's not exactly a courtroom issue. It is bigger than that. The only funny thing is that the courtrooms were the ones which moved the fastest, because privacy as a concept, particularly in cases which are sensitive in nature, whether they be criminal, or sensitive from the perspective of the facts of the case or the information behind it, is something which courts take very seriously.

But the idea is not just governing it in the courtroom. The courtroom is just possibly a first regulator, but the symptoms behind these issues are bigger than that. There's no general law governing the technology, and what we need here is not a venue-by-venue defence. What we are looking for is something along the lines of figuring out whether such kinds of things should be done in bathrooms, in casinos, in gyms, in schools, places which are sensitive in nature, in hospitals, in conference rooms of businessmen. Is this a new way of dealing with surveillance? And if yes, then is the idea to go door by door, or instead to look at the functionality of the device itself?

Now, the consent frameworks around the world are about deliberate acts. Acts which are meant to be so obvious that the collection of data is happening, and they say that we will collect data no matter what. Every consent principle around the world, be it the [GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj) or the [DPDP in India](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/06/2bf1f0e9f04e6fb4f8fef35e82c42aa5.pdf) or the [California data protection law](https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/ccpa), all of them speak about this. What they don't speak about is the non-deliberateness, or plainly, the non-daylightness of notice of privacy being infringed, which is something way more detailed than this.

Just to give an example of this, the cookie banner model has no equivalent in going to a coffee shop and having your face captured by the coffee shop's internal CCTV camera. The deliberateness makes a lot of difference in this. The intent and the nature and the purpose of the collection of data are very different in both of those cases.

Now, just to add maybe a little bit more controversy on top of this, imagine adding another layer of AI to this. That's what the Harvard kids did. They brought an FRT, PimEyes, on top of the Meta AI glasses. They said that [they will not release the technology](https://idtechwire.com/harvard-students-connect-meta-ray-bans-to-pimeyes-face-search-provoking-privacy-concerns/) because they realise how scary it is. But the idea of the project was to indicate how scarily these Meta AI glasses can be used. So they walked around the streets of Harvard and tried talking to people, and realised that within 10 seconds the FRT could put out all their records: who they are, what their names are, where they live, etc. Now imagine if someone has put this technology on a Ring camera or a dash camera. That makes it even scarier. Where do we draw a line between personal safety or personal liberty, and societal liberty and societal privacy, and the right to exist without being known that way?

I want to bring up another thing over here: a [Swedish investigation](https://techcabal.com/2026/03/04/kenya-meta-ray-ban-ai-intimate-moments/) happened this year in which subcontractors were reviewing smart glasses footage for AI training purposes. And what came out were people's homes, including their bathroom visits, their banking details. I do know that the [European Parliament formally asked the Commission](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/P-10-2026-000903_EN.html) what it intends to do with this, and the [EU Data Protection Board](https://stateofsurveillance.org/news/eu-smart-glasses-edpb-report-meta-kenya-scandal-2026/) has openly dedicated work on devices of such nature.

But this is not a problem just happening in one-off cases. There are so many cases in India, on a daily basis, where you can see people using devices for recording in industrial spaces. I do know some [industries in the south of India](https://www.aljazeera.com/gallery/2026/6/11/photos-indias-workers-are-training-ai-robots-to-take-their-jobs) are using it to record workers, to figure out how they can train an AI model on top of this. Now, this becomes a bigger concern, not just along the lines of privacy and security, but industrial use: using workers who are working in their capacity, doing manual work, to change their skills. They probably don't even realise that wearing those glasses and moving around on a daily basis is an AI model trying to copy their actions. But it is also, in some way, creating employer surveillance of employees.

Now, with this background, I also want to bring up the fact that there are laws around the world. There is a [biometric law in Illinois](https://www.ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/ilcs3.asp?ActID=3004&ChapterID=57), requiring written consent before biometric capture. And Meta has already paid out some $2 billion across [Illinois](https://www.labaton.com/news-insights/record-breaking-650-million-settlement-of-biometric-privacy-lawsuit-reached-by-labaton-keller-sucharow-edelson-robbins-geller-and-facebook) and [Texas](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/07/30/meta-agrees-to-1point4-billion-settlement-in-texas-biometric-data-lawsuit.html) for biometric claims from earlier products. California also introduced [a bill](https://calmatters.digitaldemocracy.org/bills/ca_202520260sb1130) very similar along these lines, on secret wearable recordings in businesses.

But then the issue is, how are we defining a bystander here? To me, a bystander is not the user, not the manufacturer, but somebody absolutely strange to the concept of this device or service, who is then being recorded, invading their privacy. And it's not just that simple anymore. It's not just that you're being recorded, because you were being recorded earlier also, when you're moving around in malls and airports. But now this is such an accessible resource, which can be held by both private and public entities. And not just that: if attached to an AI training model, it is a menace.

So what I really want to bring out in all of this is that you can't treat real-time facial recognition technology on consumer wearables like any other wearable or any other FRT technology. This is a combination of two things which are going to be a really big issue to deal with. This is the next form factor, and how you label glasses in a particular law, and how different laws around the world look at this.

The question, however, is: how do we deal with this? Are we looking at the functionality of these devices? Because I understand the AI innovation bit of it, and the fact that it's a great technology in itself. But at the same time, are we looking at the functionality to make it more guardrailed, to figure out how we can make sure that this does not get tampered with, etc.? Or are we looking at just the idea of the technology being scary? The question remains open, and I want to keep it open, and I want to keep it free of any personal opinion.

But the question to me is bigger than whether invisibility is something we shall now bring into the concept of recording. Like, do we even think about whether this should be a common thing in everyone's consciousness, that I may be recorded at any time, by anybody in this world, right now? It's that accessible.
