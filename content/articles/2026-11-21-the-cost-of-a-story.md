Some of the most revolutionary books, in the end, boil down to saying one and the same thing. Or perhaps it's your personal life experience that shapes the lessons you bring out of them. And of course, those experiences can be different for different people, and different for the same person at different times, reading the same book.

The interpretation, the canon event that motivated me to write this piece today, is the idea that we always have to pay for feelings if they're felt in the wrong language. Meursault feels things, but not the way society wants him to feel them, so they kill him for it. Connell and Marianne feel everything and can't say any of it. Little Dog writes to a mother who can't even read. Takako hides in a bookshop because books are the only place where feelings don't have to be performed. And Patrice Mersault commits murder to buy himself time to be happy. These people did not live a life of performance. They lived lives that did not come with a narrative or subtitles attached. They just lived. And somehow the world didn't let them.

One of my favourite authors of all time is Albert Camus. I'm a particularly big fan of *The Myth of Sisyphus*. It changed my very interpretation of life in many ways, not just personally but also professionally, and how I look at the world. I think the idea of absurdity and existentialism takes the tension out of many life decisions, which are fairly inconsequential and not as big a canon event as we make life out to be, frankly. I have read almost all of his books: *The Stranger*, *A Happy Death*, *The Myth of Sisyphus*, of course, and *The Plague*. I've also read the letters he wrote to Maria Casarès (not his wife, the actress he loved for years), published as *Letters*.

This year, I read *The Stranger* again. The last time I read it, I was 21. I think it's important to read Camus again and again (they're fairly short, but they also change as you mature). What stood out for me this year is that the person whose death led to Meursault's end is never named. He is just "the Arab". The word is used again and again throughout the book, and no one really knows who he is. I later found out that there's another book, *The Meursault Investigation* by Kamel Daoud (2013), narrated by the dead man's brother, which finally names the Arab: Musa. What stood out for me is that for 70 years we have been arguing over whether Meursault felt enough, but nobody even asked what the dead man felt. He didn't even get a name, let alone have his feelings accounted for.

What stands out for me from all of this is that we lose ourselves as readers. We don't see who gets a name, who pays, and for what.

## *A Happy Death*, Albert Camus (written 1936–38, published 1971)

I want to do this one before *The Stranger*, because Camus wrote it first. One of the most consequential sentences in this book is: "We don't have time to be ourselves. We only have time to be happy."

A bored clerk, Patrice Mersault, kills Zagreus, a rich man who uses a wheelchair, takes his money, and spends the rest of his life buying time to be happy. He dies happy, like a stone among other stones. The internet reads this as: money buys time, and time equals happiness.

But what stood out for me, having read Camus's other books as well, is that in this book he named the victim. His victim had a body, ideas, money. He was practically Mersault's teacher. When Camus rewrote the story as *The Stranger*, his other take on the absurdity of death, of murder, and of the purpose of life and crime, he changed who dies: from a named European mentor to an unnamed Arab. This revision changes a lot about how you see the story. You erase the memory attached to the victim. In *A Happy Death*, I still feel sorrow for the victim. By the time *The Stranger* comes, I don't even care about the Arab anymore. So what I need to ask out loud is: which of these versions is more universal? If we make the victim anonymous, do we kill the victim in many ways?

The second layer is that the book rests on the idea that you can inherit happiness from a corpse. Zagreus's money is the invoice, and it is quite visible in the second half of the story how much Mersault was looking forward to it. What stands out for me particularly is that in almost all of Camus's stories, as I'll get to next with *The Stranger*, his "heroes" pay for their happiness with another person's life, at the cost of another person's life. But as Camus matures, he forgets to name the person whose cost it was.

## *The Stranger*, Albert Camus (1942)

The most famous Reddit fight about this story, which you will read a thousand times, is over who is actually the hero and who is the villain. Meursault is an unusual character. He's unreadable. He's opaque. He's silent. He's unexplained. And even though the novel gives him a good hundred-plus pages to explore his inner life, we never really know who he is.

In one of the book's most famous sentences, he says: "I opened myself to the gentle indifference of the world." I don't think he ever opened himself. Yes, the world is indifferent; it doesn't really care who you are or what you need. But did he really open himself? I don't think so.

As I mentioned before, Musa, the Arab, gets a bullet but no name in the story. So Camus protects the mystery of his "hero" while denying another man even the right to exist at all. It also stood out to me that this character was an Arab, someone from a people already pushed to the margins. Meursault's opacity is a privilege in the story that has never really been highlighted. That privilege gives him the right to be private, which only Meursault gets. The other person, the Arab, Musa, gets reduced to a category. It's not the same thing. It wasn't fair.

The indifference line also makes me reread my experience of Camus's books as a whole: his heroes are never really labelled as being on the wrong side. And it is okay for a writer to do that; it's his interpretation of the world. But was the indifference really gentle to Musa? I don't think so.

## *Normal People*, Sally Rooney (2018)

From here, I move to one of my most chaotic readings of the year. It's a story I absolutely hated for many years. To be honest, I first watched the series (BBC and Hulu, 2020), and I remember being irritated by the end of it. I did not like these characters. I didn't want to relate to them, and I probably never want to. But I think the reading I did at 18, when it came out on screen, was a myopic one, with perhaps a bit of my own prejudice towards the characters based on the series I had watched. So the book never really did justice to me back then.

But when I read it this year, in August, what stood out to me was not the miscommunication or the toxicity between the characters, but the class in the romance. One of the most famous sentences in the whole story is when Connell says: "Marianne, I'm not a religious person but I do sometimes think God made you for me." It's a beautiful sentence, and personally, I felt very attached to it. What stayed with me in this reading, though, was another line: "People can really change one another."

Look at the class difference in the story. Connell's mother, Lorraine, cleans Marianne's family's house. The great love story begins in a house where the hero's mother is staff, and the tenderness is built on top of the wage she earns. The title is a joke. They're not normal people; their classes are worlds apart. They don't fit into each other's worlds. Perhaps the whole story has been trying to earn them that label, to equate them, to suggest they're normal people, but in many ways they really aren't. Marianne has the most fractured relationship with her family, something I don't think Connell will ever relate to. So they were never lovers on an equal plane. They could never grow equally.

The payback at the end is that Marianne lets Connell go to New York. She pays for his future, for what she thinks is good for him, with her own loneliness. And Sally Rooney writes that loneliness with a little hurt, and a feeling of being unworthy. Even the so-called healthy ending has an invoice attached.

Sally Rooney's lovers change each other, as she says. But something never changed between them: they never felt like equals, and they probably never will. This is one of the stories that shapes my thought that there's no such thing as right people, wrong time. If people are wrong for each other, they will be wrong no matter what, whether the time is right or wrong. And I think Connell and Marianne were wrong for each other. They were never right people at the wrong time.

## *On Earth We're Briefly Gorgeous*, Ocean Vuong (2019)

This is one of the most beautiful stories I've read in a really long time. I want to quote this sentence first: "To be gorgeous, you must first be seen, but to be seen allows you to be hunted." I remember writing in my personal diary in 8th standard that to be seen is to be vulnerable. And who really wants to be vulnerable?

The internet reading of this book is the absurdity of writing a letter to a mother who can't even read. But my reading goes back to the introduction: for some reason, names are camouflage in this story. Put it next to the Arab in *The Stranger* and it resonates. Little Dog's grandmother gave him that name on purpose. In her village, weak children were given worthless names so that evil spirits would pass them by. In Vuong's words, to love something is to name it after something so worthless it might be left alone, and alive. The Arab is unnamed in *The Stranger*, so he's erased. Little Dog is named worthless, so he's protected. Both are survival tactics of people with no power. One failed; I think the other works.

The payback in this story is that Little Dog's English, the language he writes the whole book in, was paid for by his mother's hands in nail salons. Her hands bought his words, and his words are in a language she will never be able to read. The payer can't read the receipt. The invoice is unrecognisable in this story.

So when I say that "to be seen is to be hunted" is the most precise privacy argument in contemporary fiction, I mean that being visible is not freedom. When the people looking are hunters, it can never be freedom. (I wrote about this hunter idea in one of my Soapbox pieces. [Read it here](/soapbox/blame-the-woman-save-the-ceo/).)

## *Days at the Morisaki Bookshop*, Satoshi Yagisawa (2010; English translation 2023)

This is one of the coziest, most healing and most comforting books you can possibly read. It's one of the kindest books, the kind that will probably put your heart at ease. But to me, it read like an ending with a twist. Morisaki is the only book here where the invoice is paid in kindness, and not in someone's suffering.

Takako heals in a shop her uncle inherited. The family passes down shelter, not debt. Uncle Satoru tells her the shop is a harbour, a place to rest before she sets sail again. A secondhand bookshop is literally a place where people's paid-off stories get passed on cheaply to the next reader, so it's the opposite of extraction. Every book there has already been read, loved and given up by someone, so nobody new has to bleed for it. That stands out in the line "It's important to stand still sometimes", because it asks you to wait and just look around, and you'll probably feel the kindness from there. The kindness here is that the story allows its main character to just look around.

## Closing

After four books I read this year in which someone pays in blood, wages, silence, labels and class difference, Morisaki imagines a world where the debt is passed on as kindness, and you're allowed to stand still and look around. Stories like this give us a moment to feel safe and free for a little while.

The stories we cry over are often built on someone unnamed: a Musa, a Lorraine, a mother in a nail salon. But stories like Morisaki are not meant to make you feel much, just to make you stand still. So I wonder: what is better writing? A story where the writer's personal bias is so obviously visible, with the payback, the hidden receipt and the cost of the story all there? Or a story that doesn't make you feel very much at all?
