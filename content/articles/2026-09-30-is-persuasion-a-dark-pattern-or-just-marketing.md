I was using [Flo](https://flo.health/), which is a period tracking app, earlier today, and I realised something very interesting. The interesting part is the fact that there was a sudden pop-up to subscribe, another countdown timer on a discount to see a full report on my mood swings, and another feature, maybe, another coupon to buy certain conversations with a gynaecologist, all right?

And that kind of made me think: is this like a flash sale on a bad mood of mine, because I'm going through my period right now? Is this supposed to be funny, because I can be in a bad mood and want to really see why my bad mood is fucking up my day, and just buy on this?

But then I just took a second to realise: if I was marketing this tool, if I was the one who was sitting in the sales department, what would I be doing? What would be the way I would be looking at this, right? How would I probably help get more subscriptions, or get more purchases, and get more reports being sold, and stuff like that, right?

And that seemed like a very deeply unfair question to me, because I felt like, how can simply experiencing an emotion, like a bad emotion, and some people marketing on top of it, be anything but weird and creepy, right? But at the same time, how can I create an urgency if not through a countdown? How can I create an action, unless I put that urgency in front of somebody who can probably give me an action? And what if the action is the entire job description I have, right? To get those actions in place. In principle, then, I'm not doing anything wrong. This is just a funnel that I can work through. And that's an uncomfortable place to sit in, right?

So India, via consumer protection law, has a [2023 guideline on dark patterns](https://pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1983994), which lists around 13 dark patterns, including things like false urgency and nagging and stuff like that. Now, for a thing like Flo, around two or three of them were easily textbook violations, right? And what actually lays on top of it is that it was a wellness app, right?

What actually stood out for me is: what's wrong in doing all of these things? As somebody who's expected to be an adult, what is wrong in trying to nudge me, as a marketing tool? Because there is no other way about it, right? And therefore the context matters a lot in this situation.

If I am, for example, a teenager who's probably tracking my periods, and right now it's very haphazard, I get 15 days and then five days, and suddenly I see a report nudging that, oh, you can just buy this flash sale report in the next one hour, which will be sold to you at half the cost, and it'll tell you exactly why your moods, why you have pimples right now, why your weight is increasing and stuff like that. That is worrisome. That is something which will make a teenager want to do that, because they're already in a vulnerable position.

Another example could be me being in the hospital line, and the person there telling me, oh, there's a countdown put over here, we'll give you a risk report on whether you're prone to cancer or not, based on the XYZ studies that we do. I am scared as hell right now that I might have cancer, so why would I not pay another thousand bucks to get one more report on my risk on the cancer line, stuff like that, right?

So this is just not UI/UX in that simple way. It's also leaning towards a fear response. It's urgency in a situation which is kind of vulnerable. It's using the benefit of someone's innocence, using the benefit of someone's lack of awareness, using the benefit of someone not being self-driven yet. And therefore, it doesn't just end at what exactly the tools or functionalities put in place to nudge are, but also who it is being nudged to, right?

And why is that important? To me, persuasion is not a crime. Persuasion is part of the job. I'm accidentally empathising with the growth team in this situation, or the marketing team in this situation, but I actually understand why they do it. Because there is no other way of doing it. Of course, there are better ways: smart featuring and smart marketing techniques, and things which are probably not just a freemium app of some kind, and making sure that the premium app is worth being premium for. Stuff like that. Like, sell your product.

But is persuasion equal to deception? I don't think so. I think there's some space, and some measure to be undertaken there, to understand what creativity can be like. You just can't have a toothless disclaimer being like, please design with digital awareness and a lack of dark patterns. That is dangerous for creativity, right? You're trying to ensure the team gets more paranoid and defensive and allergic to anything which has a personality of any kind.

Me putting up a cookie banner and saying, oh, please know that we are using cookies in order to, like, buy certain products from you. That's funny, that's interesting, that's creative. That might not precisely be a dark pattern, right? As somebody who has a basic understanding of reading a website, you should be able to understand that, as a reasonable, rational person. But doing that on a toddler's gaming app would be a very different perspective and point of view on the entire design model.

So what I want to say about this is how the guidelines kind of also half know this, because they also say this is a [merely indicative guideline](https://corporate.cyrilamarchandblogs.com/2023/12/dark-pattern-guidelines-illuminating-or-illusory/), right? They don't say this is binding as an instrument. The examples in the guidelines are "only guidance" and not a binding opinion, but then Guideline 5 says that engaging in any of the listed practices is a dark pattern. So even the guidelines contradict themselves. And that's not how the Central Consumer Protection Authority has acted recently, right? It has, in so many cases:

- May 2025: [a notice to Uber](https://www.medianama.com/2025/05/223-ccpa-sends-notice-uber-advance-tip-feature/) over its "advance tip" for faster pickup
- June 2025: [an advisory telling e-commerce platforms to self-audit for dark patterns within three months](https://consumeraffairs.gov.in/public/upload/admin/cmsfiles/pressRelease/Central_Consumer_Protection_Authority_issues_advisory_to_E-Commerce_Platforms_for_self-audit_within_3_months_to_detect_Dark_Patterns_and_ensure_its_resolutionpress_release.pdf); by November 2025, [26 platforms had declared compliance](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2191948&reg=3&lang=2)
- December 2025: [Zepto fined ₹7 lakh](https://www.medianama.com/2025/12/223-zepto-fined-7-lakh-dark-patterns-ahead-ipo/) for drip pricing and basket sneaking
- June 2026: [PhysicsWallah fined ₹5 lakh and McAfee ₹1 lakh](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2268302&reg=3&lang=1) for basket sneaking, confirm shaming, forced action and trick questions
- August 2026: [nine platforms penalised](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/corporate/ccpa-fines-9-digital-platforms-including-zepto-indigo-over-dark-patterns), including IndiGo, BookMyShow, FirstCry, PharmaEasy and SpiceJet
- September 2026: [Rapido fined ₹10 lakh](https://www.business-standard.com/companies/news/ccpa-fines-rapido-10-lakh-over-dark-patterns-uber-ola-under-review-126091501415_1.html) for confirm shaming and interface interference in its fare prompts

indicating that we are kind of engineering a hostage negotiation on how exactly you should look at cancellation policies, at materialising final screens, and at what exactly it would look like. That will just make everything fucking look black and white, and it will not be interesting at all.

But what I think is more important is a little bit more context-setting here. Targeting a child, a person in a health crisis, or someone who financially, desperately needs certain loans is categorically way worse, and way less on the side of persuasion than on the side of being deceptive and tricky and manipulative in nature. But having a mildly pushy subscription prompt, or having something which is slightly trying to make you want to click on a particular button to figure out what's next, is just persuasive. Occasionally annoying, yeah, sure, but also they're trying to survive here, dude.

Marketers can sometimes have the last word on this. I mean, for somebody who is genuinely irritated with the whole impact of dark patterns, I also understand why somebody would put a cancellation button in red, because it is going to be a marketing loss for them. It's engineered not purely to deceive, but also to help them. At the end of the day, marketing is a lot about carefully designing how a product shall be sold and can be sold. It's making sure that the engineers who are designing this get a platform. This is not a defence. This is a mechanism for them to function.

So, jokes apart, I think the test for persuasion versus deception was never going to be about how reasonable this sounds, but more along the lines of what is the context in which you're trying to persuade somebody, right? And yes, I'll allow the marketer a clean chit on this one.
